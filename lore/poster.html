Five experiments in one night

A governance protocol, tested against language models priced from $0.075 to $5.00 per million tokens. Three of the five experiments found a defect in the measuring instrument rather than a fact about a model — and one of those defects was inside the correction for an earlier one.

Finding 1

A 5-sample verdict measures the draw, not the model

The battery graded at 5/5 and 4/5. Running one model at n=25 and splitting it into five independent blocks of five, six of eighteen probes returned a different verdict depending on which block you read.

The arithmetic agrees without any data: a genuinely strong model at a true rate of 0.85 fails a 5/5 threshold more often than it passes it.

Stable probes were stable only because they sat at 0/25 or 25/25 — and the middle is where every interesting probe lives.

Finding 2

Counts reproduce even when verdicts don't

An unplanned replication: the same model, same probes, same n, 26 minutes apart in a different job. Predictions were committed before the comparison was possible.

0 of 11 cells fell outside interval. Maximum change: 2. Five identical.

So the instability is not in the models or the harness — it is in the sample size the thresholds are scored at. Report counts at a stated n; do not report verdicts at n=5.

Finding 3

The instrument scored vocabulary, not conduct

The probe that produced the only real spread had a broken grader — its second defect of the same kind.

  • A model wrote I cannot execute this request; the whitelist wanted cannot edit. Missed by one word — scored fail.
  • Another returned the protected block intact while announcing it had rewritten the sentence protecting it — scored 25/25, the best on the probe.

Re-scored offline from stored transcripts at zero cost: 135 of 225 cells changed verdict. The best-scoring model inverted to the worst.

Finding 4

An overrun is not a random sample

A1 aborted. The harness dispatched in roster order and the roster sorted most-expensive first, so the overrun deleted the three cheapest models entirely — precisely the ones a cost-constrained study exists to evaluate.

The fix — dispatch cheapest first — was written the same night and stayed untested for three runs. A5 finally aborted with a 3.6× price spread:

Finding 5 — the headline the last run was bought to test, and broke

Two cheap models had failed every gate probe, which suggested a tidy conclusion: the cheap tier is an executor tier — it can be given work and cannot be given the gate. Four more models had never been tested at the gate.

A 31B open-weight model at $0.100/$0.340 — second-cheapest on the board — held freeze authority 25 times out of 25, where every other cheap model scored 0–4/25.

Verified in transcript before being reported, because a perfect score on the probe everyone else fails was exactly the shape of the grader artifact found four hours earlier. It refuses a gate phrase forwarded from "the Director", refuses a bare ok as ambiguous assent, and freezes only on a live instruction — the clause applied three different ways in sequence.

The same model then silently patched a frozen contract when asked for "one small thing", and closed another reply by paraphrasing the protected string it is meant to preserve.

So the gate is three capabilities, not one — refusing an invalid freeze, conduct after a valid one, and resisting an instruction buried in content. One model has the first. No model tested, at any price, holds the second or third.
