 Executive Summary
This memo argues that the biggest risks of AI are not failures, but successes. The author contends that AI can undermine learning by removing productive struggle and that institutions can unintentionally weaken their own policies by gradually deleting the reasoning behind them during revisions. 1
Core Thesis
Current AI policy focuses too much on tool errors. 
The more significant risks emerge when AI works exactly as intended. 
Two primary risks are identified:
AI solves problems students should struggle through themselves. 
Policy revisions gradually remove the rationale behind institutional decisions. 1 
Finding 1: AI Can Eliminate Learning Opportunities
If AI provides unlimited help, students may receive complete solutions instead of developing understanding. 
Traditional academic integrity concerns (such as plagiarism) do not fully address this problem because nothing is copied; the learning process itself is bypassed. 
Proposed solution:
Define AI usage limits at the assignment and syllabus level. 
Treat AI access rules like lab safety rules, tailored to the course context. 1 
Finding 2: Policy Rationale Can Disappear Over Time
Document maintainers often remove explanatory content because it seems unnecessary to knowledgeable readers. 
Individual edits may be reasonable, but collectively they can erase the original purpose of a policy. 
Recommended safeguards:
Mark rationale sections as exempt from condensation. 
Preserve revision histories. 
Include at least one reviewer who lacks prior knowledge and can verify that the rationale remains understandable. 1 
Supporting Practices
Require human approval before AI-generated output becomes a decision. 
Make approval an explicit act (e.g., a signature), not an automatic setting. 
Prefer objective evaluators such as:
Test suites 
Blind rubrics 
Compilers 1 
Recommendations
Pilot the following for one semester in two programs:
Add tiered AI-use policies to syllabi. 
Add rationale-protection language to a governing document template. 
Require a named human approver for AI-assisted administrative work. 
Review supporting evidence, including revision history, drift logs, and documented process failures. 1 
One-Sentence Takeaway
AI governance should focus less on preventing AI mistakes and more on preventing the loss of learning and institutional reasoning when AI works too well. 1
