# The Scriptorium

The monastery received the copying engine in autumn, and by winter no one could remember how they had lived without it. Set a book on its left plate, and the right plate produced a copy in a hand more beautiful than any monk's — steadier than old Brother Anselm's, faster than young Brother Denis on his best day, and never once complaining of the cold.

The abbot, who was wise about novices, made a rule for them at once: any novice who used the engine must sit afterward with the copy and the original side by side, and read them against each other, line by line, aloud.

"The engine does not err often," he told them. "But you err often, and you will not learn to see error in its pages if you have never learned to look."

The novices grumbled. The checking took nearly as long as copying by hand once had. But they did it, because they were novices, and the rule was the rule.

No such rule was made for the senior monks. No one thought to make one. Brother Prior had copied scripture for thirty years; the cellarer's accounts had balanced for twenty. These were men whose judgment was the reason the monastery ran. To ask them to sit and check their pages like first-years would have been an insult, and besides, they were busy — the engine had freed them for more important work, and the more important work had expanded to fill the freedom.

So the Prior's letters went out unread by the Prior. The cellarer's ledgers went to the bishop bearing figures the cellarer had glanced at, admiring the handwriting. The abbot's own commentary on the Psalms — his life's work, now flowing from the right-hand plate at a pace his life had never permitted — went to the library shelf by shelf, and he blessed each volume, and did not open them.

The engine did not err often. But it erred as running water erodes: nothing on any single day, and always in the same direction — toward the smooth, the expected, the phrase that sounded like what a commentary ought to say. The novices caught these drifts in their own pages weekly. They assumed the seniors caught them too. It did not occur to them that no one above them was looking, because it did not occur to them that the rule made for their weakness was in fact a practice for anyone's — that the abbot had, without knowing it, given the children the only medicine in the house.

Years on, a novice named Colum was shelving in the library and stopped at the abbot's commentary, the famous one, and read a passage he had heard the abbot preach from memory in chapel. The page said something adjacent. Something smoother. Something no one would ever object to, which was how Colum knew the abbot had not written it, because the abbot's true sentences always had one rough edge where they gripped the world.

Colum was too junior to say anything. There was no version of the words *are you well, Father* that a novice could speak to an abbot about the abbot's own book.

So he did the only thing his training had given him. He went to the archive, found the abbot's original in the abbot's own cramped hand, carried it up, and laid it open on the reading desk beside the beautiful copy — the two pages side by side, the way he had been taught, line against line.

Then he went back to his shelving, and waited for someone with more authority to walk past the desk and look.
